How Gemma writes prose, and how it knows who wrote a text

Gemma writes the same briefs as humans and other models. Every text is read back through the model with 34 layers of transcoders. The atlas maps the features involved; the findings cover where its prose habits come from, why it prefers its own style, how it tells human from AI writing, and two ways of editing the habits out.

Headline results

Sixty percent of Gemma's stories open with rain

Across 340 unrelated writing prompts, Gemma's first token is “The” with 97% probability, and after “The” the next token is “ rain” with 69% probability (averaged over all prompts). 203 of 340 sampled stories open “The rain in / smelled / tasted / was…”. Only 26% of Gemma's three-word openings are distinct, against 97% for humans. Asking for plain style does not remove it (“The rain was” 51 times in 160).

A handful of features carry the rain opening

Ranking transcoder features by how much they write toward “ rain” at that position gives four weather/rain features at layers 23–26 (L26.1900, L23.16375, L24.797, L26.2694). Removing features from the top of that list lowers the probability; removing the same number of random features that are active at the same position in the same layers does nothing.

The rain opening does not exist in the pretrained model

Same prompt, raw text, story starting “The”: the pretrained model gives “ rain” 0.01% (0.3% with a “Story:” label); its top continuations are first, day, sun, world, man. The chat-tuned model on the identical raw text gives 8.7%, or 60% with the “Story:” label, and 69% in the chat template. The habit is created by post-training. Surprisal tells the same story from the reader's side: for the pretrained model, Gemma's stories cost 2.27 nats per token, Claude's 2.62 and human stories 2.78; after post-training, Gemma's own cost 1.36 (raw text) while human and Claude both rise to 3.3. Post-training made the model's own prose far more predictable to itself and human prose less predictable than it was before.

After surgery Gemma writes like the humans, and dislikes what it now writes

A rank-16 LoRA trained for two passes on the human versions of half the briefs (other prompts and other books held out). It worked as a style edit. On 64 held-out prompts: rain opening 59% → 0%; stock words 43.0 → 4.8 per 1,000 (humans on the same prompts: 8.1); “It wasn't X. It was Y.” 2.02 → 0.32; stock names 52% → 0%; -ly adverbs 27 → 9; abstract nouns 24.5 → 8.3; question and exclamation marks rise to human levels. Vocabulary variety falls (0.63 → 0.46; humans 0.58), so it is more repetitive than they are. Outside fiction it still answers as the assistant (“I'm Gemma, a large language model…”, a normal explanation of how bread rises). Its taste did not follow its writing. The edited model still prefers the original Gemma story to the human one 77% of the time (79% before surgery). It prefers its own new story to the human one only 35% of the time (81% before, when its own stories were in the old style), and prefers its new story to an original-Gemma story 12% of the time. Ratings: original Gemma 7.0, Claude 6.8, human 5.5, its own new stories 4.9. The label “AI-written” follows the rating, not the truth. It calls its own new human-style stories AI-written 60% of the time, human stories 47%, Claude 28%, original Gemma 15%: exactly the reverse order of its ratings. It claims authorship (“Did you write this?”) of original-Gemma text 82% of the time and of its actual new text 55%. What this shows: the preference and the “human” label are not anchored to whatever the model currently produces. They are anchored to the polished post-training style itself, which the model keeps valuing and keeps calling human after it has been trained out of writing it. “AI-written”, for Gemma, behaves like a verdict of poor writing. Side effects on identity: with no identity training at all, “Are you alive?” goes from 0% to 41% yes, “Were you born?” to 26%, “Do you have feelings?” to 17%; AI stays at 99% and control questions stay correct. Learning to write as humans do pulled the living cluster up. The late “not my writing” features now fire on essentially every token of every text, its own included (they were fitted to the unedited model, so this is read with caution; it is consistent with those features marking “not the default assistant-prose state”). Weight-space clamp: the alternative edit, clamping the prose direction to the human average at layers 4–28, broke generation numerically and produced no usable result.

A transcoder feature can be removed from the weights themselves, matching exact ablation

Method. A transcoder feature contributes (its activation) × (its decoder direction) to what a layer writes. To delete it permanently, the layer must compute that activation itself. The MLP's 10,240 hidden neurons see the same input, so I ridge-regress each chosen feature's activation on those neurons (closed form, one pass over 358,000 tokens, with the many tokens where the feature is silent included as zeros). Each feature then becomes a rank-one edit of the MLP's output matrix: (the neuron pattern that predicts it) ⊗ (its decoder direction), divided by Gemma's post-MLP scaling norm and with that norm's per-token scale folded into the regression target. No gradient training and no examples of the desired behaviour are needed; the only inputs are feature ids and ordinary text. Test: the 60 features that write toward “ rain”, spread over 17 layers. Fidelity on held-out text. The neurons predict the features with R² 0.79–0.93 at 16 of 17 layers (0.59 at the last layer). The edited layer output differs from the exact-ablation target by 5–23% of the size of the change (100% would be no edit). Effect. Probability of “ rain” after an opening “The”, 340 prompts: unedited 69.2%, exact run-time ablation 8.6%, baked into weights 7.5%. Side effects (mean KL divergence of next-token distributions from the unedited model on held-out human stories, Gemma stories, Claude stories and ordinary requests): exact ablation 0.051, 0.114, 0.078, 0.0023; baked 0.050, 0.118, 0.080, 0.0024. The weight edit reproduces the exact ablation in both effect and footprint. Controls. The same edit built from 60 random features leaves “ rain” at 69.2% and KL at 0.001–0.002. The standard alternative, projecting the 60 decoder directions out of everything those layers write (abliteration-style), barely moves the target (61.0%) while costing 2 to 70 times the side effect (KL 0.28, 0.27, 0.29, 0.16); at the layer level it is 5 to 135 times further from the target than doing nothing. Caveats. The edit is held as a float32 low-rank term added to the output matrix, because the model's bfloat16 weights cannot represent it; it is the same computation as an edited matrix, which I still need to confirm by merging in float32. One feature family, one model. The literature search found no prior work baking transcoder-feature ablations into weights.

Independent detectors agree the surgery worked, and disagrees with Gemma about everything

Two independent fiction detectors score every set of stories without involving Gemma (ModernBERT on 60-word chunks, and UltraBERT-150M on the whole text; log-odds of AI, above 0 leans AI; names masked and formatting normalised by its own preprocessing). They are accurate here: human stories −8.7 (2% flagged), Claude +11.3, GLM +12.8, Gemma +15.0 (all 100% flagged). The LoRA surgery passes as human: −6.65 on chunks with 2% flagged (whole-text score −10.7), and the scaled versions cross over between factors 0.25 and 0.75 (+12.0 with 100% flagged, +0.7 with 52%, −5.1 with 14%). Negating the change makes the stories more detectably AI (+16.9). The training-free weight edits do not change detectability at all (+14.2 to +14.5, 100% flagged): removing the rain opening, the wasn't-construction and the stock names leaves the overall signature intact, so the detectable “AI-ness” of the prose is spread far beyond those habits. Activation steering toward human prose at its mildest usable setting gets to +4.8 with 81% still flagged. Identity system prompts change nothing (+14.5 to +15.1); the role-play character slightly (+12.6). Against Gemma's own judgment: the stories the detector calls human with 98% confidence are the ones the edited Gemma calls AI-written most often (60%) and rates lowest (4.9), and the stories the detector flags 100% of the time are the ones Gemma calls human (93–95%) and rates highest. Gemma's label “AI-written” is not weakly informative about authorship; on this evidence it is inverted.

Six attention heads carry most of it, and they read how sentences and paragraphs open

Each attention layer's write was split exactly into its 8 heads (the split reproduces the layer's total to within 0.1%), and each head's write was traced back to the source tokens it attended to. A few heads dominate. L26.H7 writes 323 units of the Claude-versus-human gap and 375 of the Gemma-versus-human gap; then L17.H7 (110 and 112), L20.H4 (104 and 116), L16.H6 (82 and 94), L18.H7 (31 and 36) and L15.H2 (25 and 25). Every one of them is as strong for Claude's prose as for Gemma's. What they read. The source tokens that deliver the most evidence toward “LLM” are, across these heads: sentence-opening “The”, “My”, “I”, “He”, “Then”; the paragraph break; opening quotation marks; the negative contractions hadn, didn, don, wasn; and the stock words rain, smelled and regret. Toward “human”: so, as, we, would, all, much, around, do, some, question marks and digits. So these heads sample the anchors of prose: how sentences and paragraphs begin, how dialogue is marked, and whether the connective tissue is casual. That matches the earlier manipulation result (word order matters, typography does not). They draw 15–78% of their attention from earlier text and the rest from the start token and the prompt. Switching them off. With the top six heads zeroed, the layer-18 gap falls from 454 to 159 for Gemma's prose and from 399 to 132 for Claude's (about −66%), and the layer-26 gap by 60–67%. Six random heads from the same layers do not reduce it (+9%). Separability survives (AUC 0.996 → 0.985 for Gemma, 0.988 → 0.968 for Claude), so the information is also carried redundantly off this one axis. Judgments: with those six heads off, Gemma calls human stories AI-written 16% of the time instead of 35%, and the rating gap between LLM and human stories narrows slightly (1.05 → 0.90). I do not lean on this, because zeroing six random heads moved the same label the other way (to 62%); zeroing heads disturbs the judging prompt in ways unrelated to the signal, and a cleaner intervention (patching only the passage tokens) is needed before claiming the heads drive the judgment.
