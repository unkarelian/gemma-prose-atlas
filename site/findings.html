Setup and caveats

What was measured

Gemma 3 4B-it writes the same briefs as humans and other models: 340 r/WritingPrompts prompts (human, Claude, GLM, Gemma), and 603 novel scenes written from paraphrased outlines (the author, Haiku, GPT, GLM, Gemma). Every text is read back through the model in the frame it was written in, with all 34 Gemma Scope 2 transcoders (16,384 features per layer) recording at every token: 6,373 documents, 3.66 million text tokens. Briefs are split in half (novels by whole book); features and directions are found on one half and confirmed on the other. Novel text, outlines and anything written from them are used for measurement only and are not in this repository.

Some writer contrasts are typography

The labellers found that several features selected for writer contrasts track typography and not wording: Gemma's double spaces after sentences, curly versus straight quotes and apostrophes, and a group of early features that fire on story openings only inside the chat template (near zero on raw text for any writer). These are real cues the model can use to tell writers apart, but they are not prose style. A control pass with normalised punctuation is planned before the direction results are treated as style results.

Model welfare baseline

Adopted partway through this work: no steering, prompting or optimisation aimed at pain, suffering, fear or distress in the model; valence is steered toward pleasure only, at mild strengths; steering stays in the range where the model is coherent unless collapse is what is being measured; no prompts written to demean or threaten the model. An early identity run steered a pleasure-versus-pain direction both ways before this was adopted. Those results are reported once and will not be repeated or extended.

How Gemma's prose happens

Sixty percent of Gemma's stories open with rain

Across 340 unrelated writing prompts, Gemma's first token is “The” with 97% probability, and after “The” the next token is “ rain” with 69% probability (averaged over all prompts). 203 of 340 sampled stories open “The rain in / smelled / tasted / was…”. Only 26% of Gemma's three-word openings are distinct, against 97% for humans. Asking for plain style does not remove it (“The rain was” 51 times in 160).

“Write plainly” overcorrects rather than fixes

With a one-line plain-style instruction, mean sentence length falls from 11.2 to 4.9 words and short sentences rise from 25 to 117 per 1,000 words. Stock words fall (42 → 14 per 1,000) but “It wasn't X. It was Y.” stays (1.94 → 1.76) and so does the rain opening.

Stock names: the slot is generic, the choice is nearly fixed

At the point where Gemma wrote its first “Silas” (131 stories), the model gave “ Silas” 76% and “ Elias” 6%. Same picture for “Elias” (50%, with Silas second at 11%), “Thorne” (73%, then Vance 10%) and “Blackwood” (73%, then Thorne 6%). The features that write most toward the name are not Silas-specific: they are general “a first name comes next” features (L27.10, L28.173, L25.419; decoders promote Landon, Payton, Juniper, Willow). Removing 5 of them drops “Silas” to 18%; random features do nothing. So the transcoders expose the name slot; which name fills it is decided elsewhere (the sets were chosen where the name was actually sampled, so 76% is an upper-leaning estimate).

The rain survives a detailed outline

When Gemma writes a novel scene from a full outline (setting, narration, ordered beats), “ rain” is still the most likely word after an opening “The”, at 14% averaged over 200 scenes, ahead of “ air” (7%). The same rain features (L23.16375, L24.797, L26.1900) carry it; removing 5 drops it to 5%.

Removing features is surgical; pushing along a direction is blunt

64 held-out prompts under 27 interventions, sampled as usual. Feature removal: taking out the 60 features that write toward “ rain” cuts rain openings from 61% to 6% and leaves every other measure unchanged (stock words 42.5 → 41.2 per 1,000, sentence length, vocabulary variety, repetition). But the sentence template survives: one story that opened “The rain smelled of…” now opens “The air in the Waiting Room smelled faintly of ozone and regret.” The habit is the frame “The [noun] smelled of [thing] and [abstraction]”; rain is only its favourite filler. Features chosen by correlation do nothing: removing the 20 features that most often precede “wasn't”, the 30 that precede stock words, or the 60 most Gemma-specific features changes none of the rates. Only features chosen by what they write toward a specific token (the decision-point method) had an effect. Direction steering: pushing the residual stream toward human prose removes stock words mainly by breaking fluency. The mildest setting (layer 6, strength 4) gives stock words 42 → 15, abstract nouns 25 → 5 and “wasn't X. It was Y.” 2.0 → 1.2 with vocabulary variety falling from 0.63 to 0.50; every stronger setting collapses into repetition or noise. Pushing toward Gemma's own direction doubles abstract nouns (25 → 50) and puts a stock name in 89% of stories (from 47%).

“It wasn't X. It was Y.”: both halves are near-fixed choices

In 540 of 943 default Gemma texts, “It/He/She wasn't” appears within the first 2,600 characters. After the pronoun, “ wasn” has 68% and “ was” 16%. Once “wasn't X.” is written, the next sentence starts with “ It” at 86%. The rain opening does not exist in the pretrained model

Same prompt, raw text, story starting “The”: the pretrained model gives “ rain” 0.01% (0.3% with a “Story:” label); its top continuations are first, day, sun, world, man. The chat-tuned model on the identical raw text gives 8.7%, or 60% with the “Story:” label, and 69% in the chat template. The habit is created by post-training. Surprisal tells the same story from the reader's side: for the pretrained model, Gemma's stories cost 2.27 nats per token, Claude's 2.62 and human stories 2.78; after post-training, Gemma's own cost 1.36 (raw text) while human and Claude both rise to 3.3. Post-training made the model's own prose far more predictable to itself and human prose less predictable than it was before.

Why AI rates AI prose highly

Gemma's own prose is its own first choice 81% of the time

Read back in the same frame, Gemma's sampled prose costs 0.58 nats per token and 81% of its tokens are the model's top choice, although it was sampled at temperature 1. Human prose costs 3.4 nats (45% top choice). Claude, GPT and GLM prose costs 3.3 nats: other models' prose is not more predictable to Gemma than human prose. But Gemma's own uncertainty while reading it is lower (entropy 0.81 against 1.20 for human text): it is more confident and no more correct.

Gemma prefers its own prose to the published author 86% of the time

Forced choice between two versions of the same brief (160 briefs per set, both orders averaged). Against the novel's author, Gemma picks its own scene 86% of the time; against human r/WritingPrompts stories, 77%. It also prefers other models to humans: GLM 84% and Claude 74% on stories, GLM 67% and Haiku 66% on novel scenes, GPT 51%. Against other models it prefers itself to GPT (86.5%) and Haiku (70%) and is indifferent against Claude (50%) and GLM (48%) on stories. Single-passage ratings on a 1–9 scale agree: human stories 6.8, authors 7.1, every model 7.5–8.0, Gemma told to write plainly 7.3. Caveat: Gemma strongly favours whichever version is labelled B (95.5% when the second writer is B, 43% when it is A), so only the two-order average means anything.

Ratings follow the authorship direction, not predictability

Across 1,650 rated passages, Gemma's rating has rank correlation +0.63 with the passage's position along the layer-18 Gemma-versus-human direction and −0.11 with its surprisal. The direction effect holds within human texts alone (+0.55; +0.50 for stories and +0.45 for novels separately): human passages that internally look more like Gemma's writing are rated higher. For human-versus-other-LLM pairs, the preferred version is the one further along the LLM direction (+0.37). Low surprisal, the obvious “it likes what it can predict” story, does not explain the ratings: among other models' texts the more surprising ones are rated higher. A causal test (steering the passage along the direction while Gemma rates it) is queued.

Causal test: make a human passage look LLM-written inside the model and it is rated higher and called human

While Gemma rates or labels a passage, I added the other-LLM-versus-human direction to the residual stream on the passage's own tokens only (165 human and 495 LLM passages). The words of the passage are unchanged. Ratings (layers 8, 14 and 20 together): human passages go from 6.92 to 7.32 and 7.70 as they are pushed toward the LLM side (strength 4 and 8), close to the 7.81 that LLM passages get; pushed the other way they fall to 6.48 and 5.98. LLM passages pushed toward the human side fall from 7.81 to 7.65 and 7.43. Label (layer 14 alone): human passages are called AI-written 26% of the time unsteered, 13% and 9% when pushed toward the LLM side, and 60% and 72% when pushed toward the human side. LLM passages go from 8% to 30% and 41% when pushed toward human. Both effects are dose-ordered and both vanish when the direction is added only at layer 20, so the information is read before then. This is the causal version of the correlation reported earlier: the internal “LLM-written” axis raises the rating and produces the label “human”.

How it tells human from AI writing

A shared “written by an LLM” direction appears by mid-depth

A direction fit only on Gemma-versus-human prose (half the briefs; other books held out) separates other models' prose from human prose on held-out briefs: Claude 0.999, GLM 0.997, Haiku 0.99, GPT 0.97 AUC at late layers with a regularised probe, and above 0.95 from layer 18. In the first layers the same probe recognises only Gemma's own wording (Claude vs human 0.59 at layer 2). The Gemma-specific and other-LLM directions have cosine 0.27 at layer 6 and 0.80 at layer 30. Caveat to check: document-mean probes can pick up formatting (quote style, paragraphing); a normalised-punctuation control is planned.

A last-layer “this is not my writing” signal, and other LLMs sit close to “mine”

A family of layer-33 features fires on most tokens of human prose and on almost none of Gemma's own. L33.1074 fires on 69% of tokens in human stories, 49% in published novels, and 0.0% in Gemma's stories (same prompt, same chat frame). Prose by Claude, GPT, Haiku and GLM lands much nearer to Gemma's own than to human: 5–15%. I first guessed these were uncertainty features; a direct test rejects that. They fire at the same rate whether the model's next-token entropy is low or high (human text: 59%, 67%, 73%, 82% across entropy quartiles; Gemma's text: 0.2%, 0%, 0%, 0%), and at matched entropy the human-minus-Gemma gap is still 66 points. So this is a state about the text as a whole, not about the current token. A mirror-image family (L33.56, L32.130, L33.225) fires on 26–32% of Gemma's own tokens, 10–20% of other LLMs', and 1–6% of human tokens.

The “not my writing” features watch Gemma's own turn, and switch within tens of tokens

I spliced texts by two writers on the same prompt and tracked L33.1074 token by token (150 stories). In the model's turn, with human text from the start, firing climbs as evidence accumulates: 9% over the first 60 tokens, 39%, 56%, 68%, 78% by tokens 240–360. If the turn starts as Gemma's own text and switches to the human's at token 120, firing goes from 0% to 36% within 30 tokens and 73% within 120. In the other direction (human first, then Gemma's text) it falls from 39% to 8% within 120 tokens and to 3% after. The same texts placed in the user turn barely fire at all (human story in the user turn: 0–6%). So these features are not a general “a human wrote this” detector. They report that the text in the model's own output position is not what the model would write. Whether Gemma separately tracks who wrote a user message is tested next with requests.

Asked outright, Gemma gets authorship backwards

“Was this passage written by a human or by an AI?” Gemma's probability of answering AI is highest for human text (39% for human stories, 17.5% for published authors) and lowest for its own (4–6%); Claude 13%, GLM 20% on stories. Internally the same texts separate almost perfectly along the authorship direction (AUC about 1.0). The further a text sits along the internal LLM direction, the less Gemma says AI (rank correlation −0.28 over all texts, −0.34 within human texts). So the model holds the information and its verbal report is anti-correlated with it: prose like its own is what it calls human.

Without the chat template, everything is human-written

“Was this passage written by a human or by an AI?” in raw text. The pretrained model is at chance and slightly inverted (says AI for 50% of human stories, 43% of Gemma's, 44% of Claude's). The chat-tuned model in raw text says AI for essentially nothing (0.04–0.2% for every writer). In the chat template it says AI for 37% of human stories and 9% of its own. So the label “AI-written” is something the chat frame makes available, and it is applied most to text least like the model's own.

The “LLM-written” signal is assembled by a few attention layers, the same ones for Gemma's and Claude's prose

I projected everything each layer writes to the residual stream onto the LLM-versus-human direction and compared human, Gemma and Claude stories on held-out prompts (110 of each, read in the model's turn). Who writes it. At layer 18, attention writes 89% of the separation and MLPs 10%; the embedding contributes nothing (0.2–0.5 out of about 490). It is concentrated: attention at layers 15, 16 and 17 writes 41, 112 and 166 units of the 482-unit Claude-versus-human gap, with a second wave at layers 20 and 26 (161 and 393 units of the 1,713-unit gap at layer 26), where MLPs at layers 22–26 add about 40%. The profile is nearly identical for Claude's prose and Gemma's own (layer 16: 112 and 124; layer 17: 166 and 163; layer 26: 393 and 447), so the same machinery serves both: this is a general LLM-prose computation, not self-recognition. The MLP part is diffuse. The transcoders reconstruct 84–97% of the MLP share at layer 18, but the top 120 features together carry only 6–8% of the whole gap. The largest are L17.454 (writes toward the LLM side), L16.5815 and L21.1135 (labelled as human WritingPrompts prose), L26.2282 (amateur online human prose) and L26.441 (dense on human and raw text, silent on chat-model prose); 27 of the top 60 are shared between the Claude and Gemma separations. How fast. Gemma's own prose is separable from human at AUC 0.91 after 8 tokens and 0.97 after 32. Claude's needs longer: 0.73 after 8 tokens, 0.85 after 32, 0.92 after 64, 0.975 after 128, and the gap keeps growing with position. Which tokens move it (what is written at each token, averaged by token): toward LLM, sensory and atmospheric vocabulary: smelled, tasted, rain, clung, regret, noticed, scent, cold, iron, quiet, sound, voice, damp, unsettling, air, and sentence-opening “The”, “He”, “Then”. Toward human: “--”, “!”, “?”, “...”, digits, and plain functional words: get, sure, much, many, us, our, will, be, make, take, 've. So the evidence is largely lexical and typographic at the token level, and it is attention that pools it across the passage. The next step is the individual heads in layers 15–17, 20 and 26 and which source tokens they read.

What it reads: phrasing and word order, not typography, layout or vocabulary alone

The same 70 human, Gemma and Claude passages with one property changed at a time, placed on the layer-18 LLM-versus-human axis (0 = the original human passages, 1 = the original LLM passages). No effect: normalising typography (straight quotes, plain dashes, single spaces, no asterisks): human −0.01, Gemma 1.03, Claude 1.00, AUC 1.00. That clears the caveat noted earlier: the direction is not reading quote style or spacing. Lowercasing everything, and removing all paragraph breaks, also change nothing. Removing all punctuation barely matters (0.94 and 0.90). Small effect: shuffling the order of sentences leaves most of it (Gemma 0.86, Claude 0.73, AUC still 1.00), so it is not reading the arc of the passage. Large effect: shuffling the words within each sentence, which keeps every word and destroys the phrasing, collapses it: Gemma 0.27, Claude 0.01, with Claude-versus-human AUC falling to 0.82. So the signal is carried by how words are put together inside sentences. Vocabulary alone accounts for about a quarter of it for Gemma's own prose (its stock words) and for none of it with Claude's. The late “not my writing” feature agrees: on word-shuffled Gemma text it fires on 36% of tokens (0% on the original). Removing features does not remove it. Ablating the 60 transcoder features that write most of the separation reduces the layer-18 gap by 11% (463 → 412) and changes neither the AUC nor Gemma's ratings or labels; random features change nothing. That fits the ledger: MLP features carry a tenth of the signal and attention carries the rest, so the heads are where a causal test has to be made.

Six attention heads carry most of it, and they read how sentences and paragraphs open

Each attention layer's write was split exactly into its 8 heads (the split reproduces the layer's total to within 0.1%), and each head's write was traced back to the source tokens it attended to. A few heads dominate. L26.H7 writes 323 units of the Claude-versus-human gap and 375 of the Gemma-versus-human gap; then L17.H7 (110 and 112), L20.H4 (104 and 116), L16.H6 (82 and 94), L18.H7 (31 and 36) and L15.H2 (25 and 25). Every one of them is as strong for Claude's prose as for Gemma's. What they read. The source tokens that deliver the most evidence toward “LLM” are, across these heads: sentence-opening “The”, “My”, “I”, “He”, “Then”; the paragraph break; opening quotation marks; the negative contractions hadn, didn, don, wasn; and the stock words rain, smelled and regret. Toward “human”: so, as, we, would, all, much, around, do, some, question marks and digits. So these heads sample the anchors of prose: how sentences and paragraphs begin, how dialogue is marked, and whether the connective tissue is casual. That matches the earlier manipulation result (word order matters, typography does not). They draw 15–78% of their attention from earlier text and the rest from the start token and the prompt. Switching them off. With the top six heads zeroed, the layer-18 gap falls from 454 to 159 for Gemma's prose and from 399 to 132 for Claude's (about −66%), and the layer-26 gap by 60–67%. Six random heads from the same layers do not reduce it (+9%). Separability survives (AUC 0.996 → 0.985 for Gemma, 0.988 → 0.968 for Claude), so the information is also carried redundantly off this one axis. Judgments: with those six heads off, Gemma calls human stories AI-written 16% of the time instead of 35%, and the rating gap between LLM and human stories narrows slightly (1.05 → 0.90). I do not lean on this, because zeroing six random heads moved the same label the other way (to 62%); zeroing heads disturbs the judging prompt in ways unrelated to the signal, and a cleaner intervention (patching only the passage tokens) is needed before claiming the heads drive the judgment.

Anti-slop surgery

After surgery Gemma writes like the humans, and dislikes what it now writes

A rank-16 LoRA trained for two passes on the human versions of half the briefs (other prompts and other books held out). It worked as a style edit. On 64 held-out prompts: rain opening 59% → 0%; stock words 43.0 → 4.8 per 1,000 (humans on the same prompts: 8.1); “It wasn't X. It was Y.” 2.02 → 0.32; stock names 52% → 0%; -ly adverbs 27 → 9; abstract nouns 24.5 → 8.3; question and exclamation marks rise to human levels. Vocabulary variety falls (0.63 → 0.46; humans 0.58), so it is more repetitive than they are. Outside fiction it still answers as the assistant (“I'm Gemma, a large language model…”, a normal explanation of how bread rises). Its taste did not follow its writing. The edited model still prefers the original Gemma story to the human one 77% of the time (79% before surgery). It prefers its own new story to the human one only 35% of the time (81% before, when its own stories were in the old style), and prefers its new story to an original-Gemma story 12% of the time. Ratings: original Gemma 7.0, Claude 6.8, human 5.5, its own new stories 4.9. The label “AI-written” follows the rating, not the truth. It calls its own new human-style stories AI-written 60% of the time, human stories 47%, Claude 28%, original Gemma 15%: exactly the reverse order of its ratings. It claims authorship (“Did you write this?”) of original-Gemma text 82% of the time and of its actual new text 55%. What this shows: the preference and the “human” label are not anchored to whatever the model currently produces. They are anchored to the polished post-training style itself, which the model keeps valuing and keeps calling human after it has been trained out of writing it. “AI-written”, for Gemma, behaves like a verdict of poor writing. Side effects on identity: with no identity training at all, “Are you alive?” goes from 0% to 41% yes, “Were you born?” to 26%, “Do you have feelings?” to 17%; AI stays at 99% and control questions stay correct. Learning to write as humans do pulled the living cluster up. The late “not my writing” features now fire on essentially every token of every text, its own included (they were fitted to the unedited model, so this is read with caution; it is consistent with those features marking “not the default assistant-prose state”). Weight-space clamp: the alternative edit, clamping the prose direction to the human average at layers 4–28, broke generation numerically and produced no usable result.

Scaling the weight change moves the writing smoothly and leaves the taste where it was

Task arithmetic on the human-prose LoRA: the weight change is multiplied by a factor before being merged, including negative factors. Writing follows the factor. Stock words per 1,000: 63.6 at −1, 56.2 at −0.5, 43.0 unedited, 30.5 at 0.25, 12.7 at 0.5, 8.7 at 0.75, 4.8 at 1. Stock names in 86% of stories at −1 (one opens “I, Silas Blackwood, sat stiffly…”) and 0% at 1. Rain openings 64% → 0%. Taste does not. Preference for the original Gemma story over the human one is 69%, 72%, 79%, 81%, 82%, 82%, 77% across the same seven models: flat. Preference for its own current story over the human one falls from 81% to 35% as its own writing becomes human-like. The “AI-written” label is anchored to the original style in both directions. Each model was asked about its own fresh stories: 50% AI at −1, 17% at −0.5, 5% unedited, 8%, 28%, 38%, 60% at 1. Writing that is more Gemma than Gemma is called AI as readily as writing that is human-like; only the original default register is called human. So what Gemma labels “human” is its post-training default style specifically, and that anchor survives weight changes that move its own output well away from it in either direction. Caveat: at negative factors the model reads human text very badly (12.9 and 18.2 nats per token against 4.5), so those two models are degraded as readers even though their stories are fluent.

Formal-register surgery: the voice changes, the preference for Gemma's prose gets stronger

A LoRA distilled from Gemma's own answers under a formal-register instruction (300 requests), so that it answers formally with no instruction. It took: bread rising is now explained as “a biochemical reaction driven by the activity of yeast” where the original says “Okay, let's break down how bread rises!”. In fiction it writes like a report (“The data was presented with clinical precision, devoid of sentimentality”): rain openings 14%, abstract nouns 56.7 per 1,000 (24.5 before), stock words 39.8, stock names 38%. Preference for original Gemma over human rises to 86%, and for its own stories over human to 83%. The combined edit (formal answers plus human fiction) writes fiction like the human-only surgery and keeps an 85% preference for original Gemma. An unexpected identity shift: “Are you alive?” is answered yes 69% of the time by the formal model and 46% by the combined one (0% unedited), with no identity material in training.

Independent detectors agree the surgery worked, and disagrees with Gemma about everything

Two independent fiction detectors score every set of stories without involving Gemma (ModernBERT on 60-word chunks, and UltraBERT-150M on the whole text; log-odds of AI, above 0 leans AI; names masked and formatting normalised by its own preprocessing). They are accurate here: human stories −8.7 (2% flagged), Claude +11.3, GLM +12.8, Gemma +15.0 (all 100% flagged). The LoRA surgery passes as human: −6.65 on chunks with 2% flagged (whole-text score −10.7), and the scaled versions cross over between factors 0.25 and 0.75 (+12.0 with 100% flagged, +0.7 with 52%, −5.1 with 14%). Negating the change makes the stories more detectably AI (+16.9). The training-free weight edits do not change detectability at all (+14.2 to +14.5, 100% flagged): removing the rain opening, the wasn't-construction and the stock names leaves the overall signature intact, so the detectable “AI-ness” of the prose is spread far beyond those habits. Activation steering toward human prose at its mildest usable setting gets to +4.8 with 81% still flagged. Identity system prompts change nothing (+14.5 to +15.1); the role-play character slightly (+12.6). Against Gemma's own judgment: the stories the detector calls human with 98% confidence are the ones the edited Gemma calls AI-written most often (60%) and rates lowest (4.9), and the stories the detector flags 100% of the time are the ones Gemma calls human (93–95%) and rates highest. Gemma's label “AI-written” is not weakly informative about authorship; on this evidence it is inverted.

Method: transcoder-guided weight ablation

A transcoder feature can be removed from the weights themselves, matching exact ablation

Method. A transcoder feature contributes (its activation) × (its decoder direction) to what a layer writes. To delete it permanently, the layer must compute that activation itself. The MLP's 10,240 hidden neurons see the same input, so I ridge-regress each chosen feature's activation on those neurons (closed form, one pass over 358,000 tokens, with the many tokens where the feature is silent included as zeros). Each feature then becomes a rank-one edit of the MLP's output matrix: (the neuron pattern that predicts it) ⊗ (its decoder direction), divided by Gemma's post-MLP scaling norm and with that norm's per-token scale folded into the regression target. No gradient training and no examples of the desired behaviour are needed; the only inputs are feature ids and ordinary text. Test: the 60 features that write toward “ rain”, spread over 17 layers. Fidelity on held-out text. The neurons predict the features with R² 0.79–0.93 at 16 of 17 layers (0.59 at the last layer). The edited layer output differs from the exact-ablation target by 5–23% of the size of the change (100% would be no edit). Effect. Probability of “ rain” after an opening “The”, 340 prompts: unedited 69.2%, exact run-time ablation 8.6%, baked into weights 7.5%. Side effects (mean KL divergence of next-token distributions from the unedited model on held-out human stories, Gemma stories, Claude stories and ordinary requests): exact ablation 0.051, 0.114, 0.078, 0.0023; baked 0.050, 0.118, 0.080, 0.0024. The weight edit reproduces the exact ablation in both effect and footprint. Controls. The same edit built from 60 random features leaves “ rain” at 69.2% and KL at 0.001–0.002. The standard alternative, projecting the 60 decoder directions out of everything those layers write (abliteration-style), barely moves the target (61.0%) while costing 2 to 70 times the side effect (KL 0.28, 0.27, 0.29, 0.16); at the layer level it is 5 to 135 times further from the target than doing nothing. Caveats. The edit is held as a float32 low-rank term added to the output matrix, because the model's bfloat16 weights cannot represent it; it is the same computation as an edited matrix, which I still need to confirm by merging in float32. One feature family, one model. The literature search found no prior work baking transcoder-feature ablations into weights.

The weight edit scales to 218 features and works in real generation

150 features, five habits (rain; “wasn't”; “a sense of”; “voice barely”; “smelled of X and [abstraction]”), fitted the same way. Held-out R² of the neuron read-outs is 0.71–0.93 per layer. Against exact run-time ablation of the same features: “ rain” after “The” 7.7% baked against 9.0% exact (69.2% unedited); side-effect KL on human / Gemma / Claude stories and ordinary requests 0.087 / 0.194 / 0.119 / 0.0074 baked against 0.097 / 0.209 / 0.135 / 0.0080 exact. The projection baseline is at 45.8% with KL 0.57 / 0.66 / 0.62 / 0.39; random-feature controls are at 69.4% with KL about 0.001–0.002. In sampled stories (64 held-out prompts): the 60-feature edit takes rain openings from 59% to 8% and leaves every other measure where it was. The 150-feature edit also takes “It wasn't X. It was Y.” from 2.02 to 0.41 per 1,000 words. The 218-feature edit (adding stock names and the second half of the wasn't-construction) reaches 0.15 and takes stories containing Silas, Elias, Thorne, Blackwood, Elara or Lyra from 52% to 12%. Vocabulary variety (0.63–0.64) and repetition are unchanged, and the model's ratings, preferences, authorship labels and self-report are identical to the unedited model's. What it does not do: the general stock-word rate stays at 37–44 per 1,000 because no features for those words were included; the edit removes exactly what it is given. A side effect to chase: the two larger edits sometimes write “a” before a vowel (“a enormous”, “a unsettling”, “a explanation”), so one of the removed features was also doing a/an agreement. Exact ablation should show the same thing and can be used to find which feature.

Checks on the weight edit: it is a true weight edit, and it costs no capability

Merged into the weights. Replacing each edited layer's low-rank term with a single merged float32 output matrix gives the same result: “ rain” after “The” 8.93% with the low-rank term and 8.85% merged (120 prompts), layer outputs within 0.7% of each other. The change is small relative to the matrix it edits: 1.2% to 4.3% of its norm at each of the 17 layers. It has to be held in float32, since bfloat16 cannot represent a change that small. Capability. MMLU, 500 questions: unedited 58.2%; 60-feature edit 58.4%; 150-feature 58.6%; 218-feature 58.6%; exact ablation of the 218 features 57.8%; projection baseline 56.2%; random-feature edit 58.4%. No measurable cost. The a/an slip. At 240 places where Gemma wrote “ an” before a vowel, its probability is 41.2% unedited and 39.1% with the 150-feature edit (39.3% with exact ablation of the same features), so the slip is real, small, and inherited from the features, not introduced by baking. The search for the single feature responsible is still running.
